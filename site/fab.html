                    Gate to silicon

                    This is a NAND. This is what it looks like in silicon.

                    Pick a logic gate and a process node. The same gate is shown six ways: the logic symbol and truth table, the transistor schematic, the mask layout a designer draws with its key dimensions, a 3D view of the cell you can pull apart layer by layer, and a slice through the finished chip. The full adder and the SRAM cell are also tiled into a 4-bit adder and a small memory array. Toggle the inputs and watch which transistors turn on.

                    Gate
                    Process
                    Inputs

                    Layouts use Mead–Conway λ rules at λ = 90 nm for the planar cell , transistor widths from logical effort , and the gridded FinFET and nanosheet pitches of the node table: 51 nm gate pitch and 28 nm metal pitch for TSMC N5 , and estimated values for the 2 nm class (for comparison, IBM’s first stacked-nanosheet devices ran at 44/48 nm gate pitch and 12 nm gate length ). Finger order is an Euler path through both networks . The full adder is the mirror adder, and wide adders replace its rippling carry with look-ahead ; the SRAM cell’s pull-downs are twice as wide as its access transistors so a read cannot flip it . Cells are teaching drawings: real libraries add taps, fill and many more design rules.

                      Dimensions

                        Side by side, to the same scale

                        Every architecture, computed from its drawing

                        Effective width is the perimeter of channel the gate covers: W for a planar device, 2H + w per fin, 2(w + t) per sheet. The natural length λ = √(εSi tSi EOT / 2εox) is the double-gate form, because a tall fin and a wide sheet are both controlled mainly across their thin dimension; the longer the gate is compared with λ, the less the drain can reach the channel . A planar bulk device has no thin body, so it has no λ here. A CFET stacks the pFET on the nFET, so the pair needs one cell width instead of two .

                        Dimensions: Intel 45 nm gate pitch and gate length ; Intel 22 nm fins (8 nm wide, 34 nm tall, 60 nm pitch) and 90 nm gate pitch ; Intel 14 nm fins (42 nm tall at 42 nm pitch) and 70 nm gate pitch ; Intel 10 nm fins (7 nm wide, 46 nm tall, 34 nm pitch), 54 nm gate pitch and 18 nm gate length ; IBM’s stacked nanosheets at 44 nm gate pitch and 12 nm gate length ; the CFET follows imec’s proposal . The FinFET began as a self-aligned double-gate transistor , and the steps follow the general front-end flow . Values marked est. are drawing values, not cited figures: widths, spacer and gate heights, sheet thickness and the CFET as a whole. Equivalent oxide thicknesses are the site’s model presets.

                        What the model leaves out
                        • Every piece is a rectangular box. Real fins taper and have rounded tops, sheets have rounded corners, and source and drain epitaxy grows in faceted diamonds that merge between fins.
                        • The work-function metals, the thin interface oxide under the high-k, liners, silicide, the oxide filling around the gate and all wiring above the contacts are left out.
                        • Wells, channel doping, extension and halo implants and the anneals are not drawn; only the heavily doped source and drain are.
                        • The CFET contacts reach the top pFET only. In a real CFET the lower nFET is wired through a deep contact or from the back of the wafer, and its dimensions here are illustrative.
                        • λ and Weff are geometric estimates; drive current also depends on mobility, series resistance and the gate length actually printed.
                        Node-reading lab · measure, then match

                        Read the node from the top of a chip

                        A node’s name stopped being a real length decades ago. What you can still measure, on a die with its upper layers polished away, is how often things repeat: the gate pitch, the fin pitch, the tightest metal pitch and the size of one memory cell. Measure them on a mystery chip, and the lab finds which node made it.

                        How to measure

                        Put mark A on the centre of one line and mark B on the centre of a line several pitches further on, then set how many pitches lie between them. Click the picture to move the nearer mark. Each mark can be off by one step of this microscope, so a single pitch is uncertain by √2 steps; across ten pitches the error is ten times smaller.

                          What the model leaves out
                          • The pictures are drawn, not photographed. Real micrographs show rounded line ends, cut gates, contacts, dummy fins and many cell types side by side; you have to find a regular stretch before you measure.
                          • The line widths and the bitcell’s shape are drawing choices. The bitcell is drawn two gate pitches wide, with its height set by the published area.
                          • One node has several pitches. Libraries for speed use a wider gate pitch than libraries for density, and each metal layer has its own pitch; the table gives the tightest published figures.
                          • The 45 nm metal pitch is an estimate. For N5, write-ups disagree on the metal pitch (28 nm from TSMC’s stated 30 % shrink, 36 nm in others); the lab uses 28 nm.
                          • To see these layers on a real chip you remove the layers above them; that is lab work with hazardous chemistry and is not described here.

                            What the model leaves out
                            • The pictures are drawn from the models’ numbers, not photographed. Real defects are found by inspection tools among millions of good features, and many need an electron microscope and a cross-section to see.
                            • Each model is the one in its process theatre, with teaching values for rates, sticking and reaction probabilities (marked est. there). The sizes are chosen so exactly one feature of four fails.
                            • The particle sizes are fixed points of the 1/d³ size distribution, not a random draw, and only one particle lands on each pair of lines.
                            • The electromigration void time is for a long line at a fixed current; real lines see varying current, heat from their neighbours and a spread of lifetimes.
                            • Real defects often have several causes at once, and fabs find them by correlating maps from many wafers. Finding a defect on a real chip often means removing layers; that is lab work with hazardous chemistry and is not described here.
                            Virtual Fab · computed cross-section

                            Write your own traveler

                            Add process steps in the order you think a fab would run them. Each step is checked against the steps it needs; a HOLD stamp means something is missing before it. Each step that passes runs the model behind its process theatre, at the theatre's default settings, at the settings you chose in the theatres above or at the recipe of a real 2024 cleanroom run, and the cross-section below is computed from the results: oxide grown by Deal–Grove, junctions from the implant, diffusion and anneal models, metal polished by Preston's law. Every hot step after doping keeps moving the junctions already made, and oxidation eats into the silicon above them, so the order of the furnace steps matters: that is the thermal budget.

                              Other process families · comparison

                              Not every chip is made like this one

                              The same few operations, grow, pattern, dope, deposit and wire, are combined very differently depending on the material and what the chip has to do. Silicon carbide needs anneals hotter than any silicon furnace, while oxide transistors for displays are made at room temperature on plastic. Each cell carries its source; a dash means no published value we could cite, as mask counts of commercial processes are not public.
