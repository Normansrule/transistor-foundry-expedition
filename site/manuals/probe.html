MANUAL 11 · AREA 11 · 7 OPERATIONS

Find the good dies and package them

Back end: probe test structures, test every die, map, saw, pick, bond and test again

A finished wafer holds anything from a handful to hundreds of chips, and some are dead. Probing finds them, a saw cuts the wafer apart, and only the good dies are packaged and wired to the outside world. No test catches every fault, so the last question is how many bad chips slip through.

Yield follows Stapper’s negative-binomial model1 and Murphy’s cost argument2; escaped defects follow Williams and Brown3; the teaching-run results come from its electrical characterization4. The probe theatre computes all of it.

OP 11.01

Probe the test structures

Lower needles onto the test resistors, capacitors and transistors beside the dies and measure them first. They tell you whether the process worked before any chip is tested.

  • teaching run: 8 of 9 test MOSFETs switched4
  • test structures sit in the scribe lanes

Check Threshold voltages and sheet resistances are inside their limits.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolWafer prober with a parametric analyser
PurposeCheck the process before the product
Working MOSFETs, teaching run8 of 94
ControlDevice parameters against limits
Out of controlParameters out of range: hold the lot and tell process engineering

Process engineer

Parametric tests measure the process, not the design. In the teaching run they showed thresholds pulled negative by charge in the oxide, and one short-channel device that never turned off4.

OP 11.02

Test every die

Step the wafer under the probe card die by die and run the test patterns. Each die passes or is put in a failure bin.

  • fault coverage: the share of modelled faults the patterns catch3

Check Every die has a result.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolWafer prober and automatic tester
PurposeSort good dies from bad
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlFault coverage and test time per die
Out of controlA whole region fails: look for a tool problem, not random defects

Process engineer

Scan chains let a few pins reach millions of internal flip-flops, so patterns can be generated automatically for a fault model. Test time costs money, so patterns that fail bad dies early run first3.

3

OP 11.03

Make the wafer map

Colour each die by its result. The pattern of failures says as much as their number.

  • Y = (1 + A·D₀/α)^(−α)1
  • big dies catch more defects2

Check The map is saved with the lot record.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolWafer map software
PurposeRecord yield and failure patterns
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlYield per wafer and per lot
Out of controlRings, scratches or clusters: trace them to a tool

Process engineer

Y = (1 + A D₀ / α)^(−α)

Defects cluster, so the negative-binomial model with a clustering parameter α fits real wafers better than a random scatter1. Murphy showed in 1964 that yield and die size together set the cost of a chip2.

OP 11.04

Saw the wafer apart

Mount the wafer on tape in a frame and cut along the scribe lanes with a diamond blade. The dies stay on the tape.

  • the cut destroys the test structures in the lanes
  • some wafers are cut by laser instead

Check Clean cuts; no chipped die edges.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolDicing saw with water cooling
PurposeSeparate the dies
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlKerf width and edge chipping
Out of controlChipped edges: slow the feed or change the blade

Process engineer

Cooling water carries heat and debris away. An edge chip that looks harmless can grow into a crack once the die is stressed in its package.

5

OP 11.05

Pick the good dies

The picker reads the wafer map and lifts only the dies that passed. Each is inspected before it goes into a tray or onto a package.

  • cost per good die is wafer cost divided by good dies2

Check Only passing dies leave the tape.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolDie picker with vision
PurposeKeep only known-good dies
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlMap-to-die matching
Out of controlPicker and map disagree: stop and re-align

Process engineer

Chiplets and 3D stacks join several dies in one package, so a single bad die wastes all the good ones beside it: every die must be known good before it is assembled5.

OP 11.06

Bond wires to the pads

Glue the die into its package, then join each pad to a package lead with a fine wire.

  • gold or copper wire, welded by heat and ultrasound
  • advanced packages use bumps instead5

Check Every wire passes a pull test on samples.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolWire bonder
PurposeConnect the die to the outside
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlBond pull and shear strength
Out of controlWeak bonds: clean the pads and recheck the bond settings

Process engineer

Wire bonds have joined chips to packages since the planar era. Modern high-performance packages replace them with solder bumps or direct copper-to-copper bonding between stacked dies5.

OP 11.07

Test again, packaged

Test the packaged part at speed and temperature. Assembly can damage a die, and some faults are only reachable through the package.

  • defect level DL = 1 − Y^(1 − T)3
  • 90 % coverage lets about ten times more bad parts through than 99 %3

Check The part passes, and is marked and shipped.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolFinal tester and burn-in ovens
PurposeShip only good parts
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlDefect level in parts per million
Out of controlEscapes at the customer: raise test coverage

Process engineer

DL = 1 − Y^(1 − T)

Williams and Brown showed that the share of bad parts that pass the test depends on both yield and fault coverage, DL = 1 − Y^(1 − T)3. The probe theatre lets you change both.

The numbers

How many bad chips slip through

Yield says how many dies are good. Test coverage says how many of the bad ones you catch.

  • Murphy related die size to yield and cost in 19642
  • Stapper modelled defect clustering with the negative binomial in 19731
  • Williams and Brown linked fault coverage to the defect level customers see in 19813
  • Teaching run: 8 of 9 test MOSFETs switched; the shortest never turned off4

What the models leave out

  • Every defect is treated as a killer; real tests bin dies by speed and repair memory with spare rows.
  • The defect-level formula assumes faults are equally likely and independent.
  • Saw, pick and bond are drawn, not modelled; their own yield losses are left out.
  • Burn-in, reliability screens and packaging stress are only mentioned.
