MANUAL 4B · AREA 04 · 8 OPERATIONS

Make the photomask

Mask making: blank, write, develop, etch the chrome, strip, measure, inspect and repair, mount the pellicle

Before a wafer can be patterned, the pattern has to exist as a mask: a plate of glass with a thin opaque film where light must be blocked. A mask is written once, checked obsessively and then used to print thousands of wafers. A device can need between 5 and 40 or more of them.

The sequence follows public descriptions from two mask makers1,2, with mask types and numbers from the Photomask article3.

OP 4B.01

Start with a blank

Take a plate of fused silica (quartz) or soda-lime glass coated with chrome, an anti-reflective layer and resist.

  • fused silica (QZ) or soda-lime glass (SL)1
  • reticles are typically quartz1

Check No scratches, pinholes or particles on the blank.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolBlank stocker
PurposeSupply a defect-free plate
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlBlank defect map from the supplier
Out of controlDefect on blank: reject it

Process engineer

Quartz transmits deep-ultraviolet light and barely expands with heat, so it is used for reticles in steppers and scanners; soda-lime glass is cheaper and serves for copy masks and contact printing1. Chromium is used because it blocks ultraviolet light well3.

2

OP 4B.02

Write the pattern

A laser or electron beam scans the layout into the resist, piece by piece, straight from the design file.

  • laser writer with a micromirror array, 0.8 µm pixels2
  • leading-edge masks: electron-beam writers4

Check The writer logs every field as written.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolLaser or electron-beam mask writer
PurposeTurn the layout into a latent image
Laser-writer pixel (example)0.8 µm2
ControlWriter calibration; stitching marks
Out of controlStitching error between fields: rewrite on a new blank

Process engineer

No mask exists yet to copy from, so the pattern is drawn directly. A laser writer flashes frames through a digital micromirror device and stitches them together2. Masks for advanced nodes are written with electron beams4.

OP 4B.03

Develop

Dissolve the written resist so the chrome shows through where the pattern must become clear.

  • developer: institution-only handling

Check Openings are clean down to the chrome.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolMask developer
PurposeOpen the resist
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlVisual and CD check
Out of controlScum: rework or reject

Process engineer

This is the same chemistry as wafer lithography (Manual 4A), on a glass plate instead of silicon1.

4

OP 4B.04

Etch the chrome

Remove the chrome through the resist openings so light can pass there.

  • a chemical process that removes chrome selectively2
  • chrome etchant: institution-only

Check Clear areas are fully transparent.

Operation specification · Tier 3 · institution-only
ToolMask etch station
PurposeTransfer the pattern into the chrome
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlClear-area transmission; CD
Out of controlChrome left in openings: re-etch or reject

Process engineer

The chrome etchant is hazardous chemistry handled only by trained staff, so this step is conceptual here. Advanced masks are etched in a plasma instead, for the same reason wafers are: straighter edges4.

OP 4B.05

Strip the resist

Remove the remaining resist. What is left is the chrome pattern on glass.

  • chrome on quartz or soda-lime, with the anti-reflective coating2

Check No resist residue.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolResist strip and clean
PurposeLeave a clean chrome-on-glass pattern
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlParticle count after clean
Out of controlParticles: clean again

Process engineer

The plate now works like a stencil: it blocks light where chrome remains and passes it where chrome was etched.

OP 4B.06

Measure the critical dimensions

Measure line widths at many points and check them against the design.

  • CD measurement1

Check Every measured width is inside its tolerance.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolMask CD metrology
PurposeProve the mask prints the right size
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlCD mean and uniformity
Out of controlOut of tolerance: reject the mask

Process engineer

A reticle used at 4× reduction prints features four times smaller than it carries3, so a mask error also shrinks by four on the wafer.

OP 4B.07

Inspect and repair

Compare the whole mask against the design to find any defect, and repair it if possible.

  • inspect, then repair if necessary1

Check No printable defect remains.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolMask inspection and repair tools
PurposeMake the mask defect-free
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlDefect list after inspection
Out of controlUnrepairable defect: rewrite the mask

Process engineer

One defect on a mask repeats on every die it prints, on every wafer. That is why masks are inspected far more strictly than wafers. A small chrome spot (drawn here between the lines) would be removed or patched by repair tools.

OP 4B.08

Mount the pellicle

Glue a frame over the pattern with a thin transparent film stretched across it. Dust now lands on the film, out of focus.

  • a protective pellicle is attached last1
  • pellicle film on a frame glued over one side3

Check The pellicle is flat and free of particles.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolPellicle mounter
PurposeKeep particles out of focus
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlParticle check through the pellicle
Out of controlParticle under the pellicle: remove, clean, remount

Process engineer

A particle sitting on the pellicle is millimetres above the pattern, far outside the depth of focus of the projection lens, so it does not print3.

Mask types

From contact masks to EUV reticles

As features shrank, masks changed from simple chrome stencils to precision optical parts.

  • Steppers and scanners project the mask pattern shrunk four or five times3
  • Reduction ratios on offer include 1:1, 2.5:1, 4:1 and 5:11
  • Features of about 150 nm and below generally need phase-shifting masks3
  • EUV masks reflect light from many alternating molybdenum and silicon layers3,5
  • One high-end phase-shift mask could cost up to $100,000 in 20063
  • A functioning device can need between 5 and 40+ masks1

What the models leave out

  • Optical proximity correction and inverse lithography reshape the drawn features on the mask; they are not drawn here.
  • Mask writing times, data volumes and write strategies are only mentioned.
  • Chrome etch and resist strip chemistry are named but never given as procedures.
