MANUAL 13 · AREA 13 · 6 OPERATIONS

Make a planar transistor the 1959 way

Hoerni’s planar process: grow oxide, open a window, predeposit, drive in under the oxide, evaporate leads, test breakdown

Before 1959 transistors were mesas: bumps etched out of the silicon with their junctions exposed at the sides, where dirt and moisture spoiled them. Jean Hoerni kept the oxide on. Dopant went in through a window, spread sideways under the oxide edge and ended under protecting glass, and the wafer stayed flat. Robert Noyce then ran aluminium leads over that oxide, which made the integrated circuit possible.

The sequence follows Hoerni’s patent1 and Noyce’s lead structure2, with oxide masking from Frosch and Derick3; breakdown of the flat junction and its curved edge follows Sze and Gibbons [@sze_gibbons1966; @sze_gibbons_curv1966], as computed by the Fab Journey’s planar theatre; the history follows Riordan and Hoddeson4.

OP 13.01

Grow the masking oxide

Oxidise the whole wafer in a furnace. The glass will block the dopant everywhere except where a window is opened.

  • silicon dioxide masks the common dopants3
  • a grown oxide also quiets the silicon surface5

Check Oxide thickness even across the wafer.

Process engineer

Frosch and Derick found in 1957 that a grown oxide blocks boron and phosphorus, and Atalla showed that it also stabilises the silicon surface [@frosch1957; @atalla1959]. Hoerni put both to work.

OP 13.02

Open a window

Print a window with photoresist and etch the oxide away inside it. Its edge sets where the junction will meet the surface.

  • the window edge decides where the junction surfaces1

Check Window clean to the silicon, edges sharp.

OP 13.03

Predeposit the dopant

Trained staff run the dopant source in a furnace. A thin, heavily doped skin forms in the window only.

  • the oxide stops it everywhere else

OP 13.04

Drive in under the oxide edge

Heat again with the source removed. The dopant spreads down and sideways, so the junction curves up and meets the surface under the oxide, not in the open.

  • the junction ends under protecting oxide1
  • sideways spread is about 0.8 of the depth6

Check Junction depth on a bevelled monitor on target.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolDiffusion furnace
PurposeBury the junction edge under glass
Drive-in, 1 h at 1100 °C on 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ (model)xⱼ ≈ 2.05 µm6
ControlDrive-in temperature and time
Out of controlJunction too shallow: the edge curves sharply and breaks down early

Process engineer

Because the dopant moves sideways about as far as down, the junction reaches the surface beneath the oxide edge. That is the whole trick: the edge, where mesa transistors failed, is sealed from the start [@hoerni1962; @riordan1997].

OP 13.05

Evaporate aluminium leads over the oxide

Open contact holes, evaporate aluminium over the whole wafer and pattern it, so the leads run across the oxide to the junctions.

  • leads over the oxide connected devices on one chip2

Check Leads continuous; contacts ohmic.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolVacuum evaporator, aligner, aluminium etch
PurposeWire the device without touching bare silicon
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlLead continuity; contact resistance
Out of controlOpen lead over the oxide step: thicker metal or a gentler step

Process engineer

Because the planar wafer is flat and covered in glass, a metal film can run over it from one device to the next. Noyce’s 1959 patent did exactly that, and the integrated circuit followed2.

OP 13.06

Test the breakdown

Reverse-bias the junction on a curve tracer and find the voltage where current suddenly rises. The curved edge breaks down before the flat floor.

  • the curved edge breaks down first8
  • 1 h at 1100 °C: flat 347 V, edge 150 V in the model7

Check Breakdown above the specification.

Operation specification · Tier 1 · care
ToolCurve tracer
PurposeConfirm the junction holds its voltage
Flat junction on 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ (model)≈ 347 V7
Curved edge, 2.05 µm deep (model)≈ 150 V8
ControlBreakdown voltage; leakage before breakdown
Out of controlEarly breakdown: shallow junction or a damaged edge

Process engineer

The field crowds at the curved edge, whose radius is about the junction depth, so it avalanches before the flat floor. A deeper drive-in gives a gentler curve: four hours at 1150 °C raise the edge from 43 to 60 % of the flat value in the planar theatre8.

Why it mattered

From mesa to planar to the integrated circuit

Keeping the oxide on made transistors reliable, flat and wireable on one chip.

  • Frosch and Derick found in 1957 that silicon dioxide masks dopant diffusion3
  • Hoerni filed the planar process in 19591
  • Noyce filed aluminium leads over the oxide in 19592
  • A grown oxide stabilises the silicon surface5

Institution-only Diffusion sources are institution-only and furnaces run near 1100 °C; every step here runs in a staff-operated lab.

What the models leave out

  • The junction edge is a cylinder of radius equal to the depth; real edges also have corners, which break down even earlier.
  • Breakdown uses Sze and Gibbons’ formulas for a one-sided abrupt junction; a diffused junction is graded.
  • No dopant source or furnace setting beyond the model example is given: the source is institution-only.
  • Surface charge in the oxide, which troubled early planar devices, is described, not modelled.
