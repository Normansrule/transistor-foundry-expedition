MANUAL 3 · AREA 03 · 6 OPERATIONS

Grow glass on the silicon

Thermal oxidation: load, ramp, oxidise dry or wet, anneal, unload, measure

Silicon makes its own insulator. Heat it in oxygen or steam and a layer of silicon dioxide grows from the surface down, part of it made of silicon taken from the wafer itself. That glass is the best-behaved interface any semiconductor has, and it made the planar process and the MOSFET possible.

Growth follows the Deal–Grove law1 with the rate constants used across the site2; furnace practice follows Plummer, Deal and Griffin3; the recipes and thicknesses come from a four-mask NMOS run in a university teaching cleanroom4.

OP 3.01

Load the boat

Stand the wafers in a quartz boat with a monitor wafer at each end and push it slowly into the furnace while it idles under nitrogen.

  • load straight from cleaning3
  • slow push avoids warp and slip

Check Every slot logged; monitors in place.

Process engineer

A wafer that waits after cleaning collects a native oxide and particles that would be sealed under the new film. Monitor wafers at both ends of the load show whether every position saw the same heat3.

OP 3.02

Ramp up in nitrogen

Raise the furnace to the oxidation temperature under nitrogen, so no oxide grows until the temperature is steady.

  • the rate depends exponentially on temperature1

Check All zones read the set point.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolTube furnace with profiled zones
PurposeReach a stable temperature first
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlZone temperatures

Process engineer

Both Deal–Grove rate constants follow Arrhenius laws, so a few degrees of error changes the thickness measurably1,2. Ramp rates and set points are part of every qualified recipe.

OP 3.03

Grow a gate oxide in dry oxygen

Switch the gas to dry oxygen. A thin, dense oxide grows slowly and evenly from the surface down.

  • teaching run: 1100 °C for 60 minutes gave 114 nm4
  • Deal–Grove predicts 111 nm1,2

Check The monitor wafer will tell; nothing to see yet.

Process engineer

x² + A x = B (t + τ)

Oxygen diffuses through the oxide already grown and reacts at the silicon below. A thin film grows linearly, at B/A; a thick one slows to x² = Bt1. For every nanometre of oxide, 0.44 nm of silicon is consumed3.

OP 3.04

Grow a field oxide in steam

For thick isolation, run a short dry step, a long step in steam, and a short dry step again. Steam carries far more oxidant through the oxide, so it grows many times faster.

  • teaching run: dry–wet–dry, 5–90–5 minutes at 1000 °C4
  • teaching run: 548 nm measured, 517 nm by Deal–Grove4

Check The colour of the monitor wafer has changed through several orders of interference.

Process engineer

The dry steps give a clean interface underneath and a dense skin on top; the wet step does the bulk of the growth. Earlier runs of the same recipe gave 445 and 522 nm5, so run-to-run spread is part of the lesson.

OP 3.05

Anneal in nitrogen

Switch back to nitrogen and hold the temperature for a while. The oxide does not grow, but its charge settles.

  • lowers the fixed charge near the interface3

Check Gas switched; hold time logged.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolTube furnace
PurposeReduce fixed oxide charge
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlAnneal time and ambient
Out of controlHigh fixed charge on C–V tests: lengthen the anneal

Process engineer

Charge left in the oxide near the interface shifts the threshold voltage of every transistor built on it. In the teaching run that charge pulled the thresholds negative, which the Process Lab works out from the measured values6.

OP 3.06

Unload and measure

Ramp down slowly, pull the boat out, and measure the monitor wafers at several points with an ellipsometer or a reflectometer.

  • a colour chart gives a quick first estimate
  • silicon consumed: 0.44 nm per nm of oxide3

Check Mean and spread inside the limits for the recipe.

Process engineer

Ellipsometry fits the change in polarisation of reflected light to thickness and refractive index. A gate oxide is also tested electrically: capacitance against voltage gives its thickness, charge and interface quality3.

Compare

Dry oxygen versus steam

Dry oxide is slow and dense, for gates. Steam is fast, for thick isolation and masks.

  • Grown oxide masks silicon against dopants7
  • Thermal oxide passivates the silicon surface8
  • Deal and Grove’s linear-parabolic law covers both dry and wet growth1
  • Teaching run: the model lands within 6 % of the measured gate and field oxides4

What the models leave out

  • Deal–Grove underestimates very thin dry oxides, which grow faster at the start than the law predicts.
  • The steam source is treated as one atmosphere of water vapour; a weak source gives less.
  • Stress, orientation effects at edges and dopant-enhanced oxidation are not drawn.
  • Furnace times and temperatures here are one teaching run’s values, not a qualified recipe for any tool.
