MANUAL 5 · AREA 05 · 9 OPERATIONS

Carve the pattern into the film

Plasma (reactive ion) etch: load, pump down, flow gas, strike plasma, etch, endpoint, over-etch, strip, measure

Lithography only draws the pattern in resist. Etching copies it into the film underneath: oxide, polysilicon, nitride or metal. A wet etch dissolves the film in liquid and eats sideways as fast as down. A plasma etch fires ions straight down, so the walls stay vertical and small features survive.

Tool facts come from public university cleanroom pages1,2. The wet-versus-dry comparison uses measured widths from a four-mask NMOS run in a university teaching cleanroom3.

OP 5.01

Load the patterned wafer

Place the wafer on the lower electrode of the etcher. The hard-baked resist covers everything that must stay.

  • parallel-plate RIE, 13.56 MHz1

Check Resist edges are sharp and the openings are clean.

In a parallel-plate reactive ion etcher the wafer sits on the powered electrode. The BYU tool is an Anelva DEM-451 with a 14-inch cathode1.

OP 5.02

Pump down

Close the chamber and pump the air out until the pressure is very low.

  • pump to 20 mTorr or less first1
  • ultimate pressure mid 10⁻⁷ Torr on a turbo-pumped tool2

Check The gauge reads the base pressure before any gas flows.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolVacuum pumps and capacitance gauge
PurposeRemove air and water before the process gas
Base pressure before etch≤ 20 mTorr1
ControlBase pressure and leak-up rate
Out of controlWill not pump down: check the door seal, then call staff

Process engineer

Air and water vapour would react in the plasma and change the etch. Pumping first also makes the gas mixture repeatable from run to run. The tool’s guide warns never to switch on the RF power while the chamber is at high pressure1.

3

OP 5.03

Flow the process gas

Let a measured flow of etch gas into the chamber. Fluorine-based gases etch silicon, oxide and nitride.

  • CF₄ with O₂ for Si, Si₃N₄ and SiO₂1
  • gas supply is institution-only

Check Flows and pressure settle at the recipe values.

Operation specification · Tier 3 · institution-only
ToolMass-flow controllers, gas cabinet
PurposeSupply the reactive species
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlFlow readings and chamber pressure
Out of controlFlow will not stabilise: abort the run and call staff

Process engineer

The gas is chosen for the film: fluorine chemistries for silicon and its oxide and nitride1, chlorine for aluminium and compound semiconductors2. Gas cylinders, cabinets and exhaust scrubbing are run by trained staff, so this manual names the gases but gives no handling procedure.

4

OP 5.04

Strike the plasma

Switch on the radio-frequency power. The gas glows: it has become a plasma of ions and reactive fragments.

  • 13.56 MHz at a few hundred watts4
  • pressure rises 20–40 mTorr when RF turns on1
  • reflected power 1–10 W1

Check A steady glow, and reflected power stays small.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment

Process engineer

Electrons pick up energy from the RF field and break gas molecules into ions and radicals. Light electrons charge the wafer electrode negative, a DC self-bias of typically a few hundred volts4. The RF supply is high voltage, so the chamber is interlocked.

5

OP 5.05

Etch straight down

Ions accelerate across the sheath and strike the wafer head-on. Where the resist is open the film is etched downward, and the walls stay vertical.

  • anisotropic unless run in a high-pressure, purely chemical mode1
  • example: GaAs at 2 mTorr Cl₂, 350 V bias, about 200 nm/min2

Check The film thins only in the openings.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolReactive ion etcher
PurposeTransfer the pattern into the film
Example rate (GaAs, Cl₂)≈ 200 nm/min at 2 mTorr, 350 V2
ControlEtch time against the measured rate on a monitor
Out of controlSloped walls: lower the pressure so ions dominate

Process engineer

R(AR) = R₀ / (1 + ¾·s·AR)

Ion bombardment speeds the chemical reaction only on surfaces the ions hit, the floor of each opening, so the etch is anisotropic4. Narrow openings etch more slowly than wide ones, because fewer reactive molecules find their way down a deep, narrow hole5. The Fab Journey’s etch theatre simulates this lag.

OP 5.06

Stop at the layer below

Watch for the moment the film clears and the layer underneath shows. That is the endpoint.

  • selectivity: film rate ÷ stop-layer rate
  • CF₄/O₂ rate falls from Si to Si₃N₄ to SiO₂1

Check Every opening is clear down to the silicon.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolReactive ion etcher with endpoint detection
PurposeStop on the underlying layer
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlEndpoint signal or timed etch from monitor wafers
Out of controlEtched into the silicon: lower power, or choose a more selective chemistry

Process engineer

Selectivity is the ratio of etch rates of the film and the layer that must survive. In a plain CF₄/O₂ plasma silicon etches faster than nitride, and nitride faster than oxide1, so when oxide is etched down to silicon the silicon is the faster one: keep the over-etch short. Production oxide etches use other fluorocarbon chemistries to protect the silicon.

OP 5.07

Over-etch a little

Keep etching for a short extra time so thin spots and slow, narrow openings clear too.

  • strong loading effect: more open area etches slower1

Check No film is left in any opening, even small ones.

Operation specification · Tier 2 · heat or equipment
ToolReactive ion etcher
PurposeClear the slowest openings
Set by the tool program; no public number to cite.
ControlOver-etch as a percentage of the main etch
Out of controlResist eroded through: thicker resist or a hard mask

Process engineer

The etch rate depends on how much of the wafer is open, the loading effect1, and on how narrow each opening is5. Over-etch covers both. It also erodes the resist, which is why deep etches use a hard mask of oxide or nitride instead.

OP 5.08

Strip the resist

Burn the leftover resist off in an oxygen plasma, then clean the wafer.

  • oxygen plasma ashing6

Check No resist residue under the microscope.

ToolPlasma asher
PurposeRemove the resist mask

Process engineer

An oxygen plasma turns the organic resist into carbon dioxide and water vapour6. Wet strippers are also used, but they are institution-only chemistry.

OP 5.09

Measure the result

Measure the etched lines and compare them with the drawn widths and with the resist before etch.

  • teaching run, wet oxide etch: openings grew 2.48 µm on average3

Check Etch bias (etched width minus resist width) is inside its limit.

Operation specification · Tier 0 · safe
ToolOptical microscope, CD-SEM, profilometer
PurposeAccept, rework or scrap
Wet-etch bias, teaching run (ACTV)+2.48 µm mean3
ControlLine width, etch depth, profile
Out of controlBias too large: shorten the etch or switch from wet to dry

Process engineer

A wet etch undercuts the resist by about the film thickness on each side, plus any over-etch. In the teaching run the openings in the field oxide came out 2.48 µm wider on average than the resist openings3. A plasma etch keeps that bias near zero, which is what makes nanometre features possible.

Compare

Wet etch versus plasma etch

Both remove film through the resist openings. The difference is direction: a liquid etches in every direction, a plasma etches mostly down.

  • Wet chemical etching gives typically isotropic profiles; RIE gives very anisotropic ones4
  • Teaching run: wet-etched oxide openings came out 2.48 µm wider than the resist on average3
  • Teaching run: polysilicon gates came out 1.34 µm narrower after their wet etch3
  • Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) sources reach very high plasma densities; a separate RF bias on the wafer then sets the ion energy4
  • Deep reactive ion etching (the Bosch process) alternates etch and passivation to cut deep vertical trenches4
  • Narrow openings etch slower than wide ones (aspect-ratio-dependent etching)5

Institution-only Wet etchants for oxide (hydrofluoric acid and buffered oxide etch) and for polysilicon are among the most dangerous chemicals in a cleanroom. They are named here only to explain the comparison. No concentrations or procedures are given.

What the models leave out

  • Etch rates depend on the exact tool, gas mix, power and loading; the numbers here are examples from public tool pages, not a qualified recipe.
  • Polymer build-up on sidewalls, plasma damage and charging are not drawn.
  • Atomic layer etching and high-aspect-ratio memory etches are only mentioned.
  • Gas handling and wet chemistry are named but never given as procedures.
