# 24 · The Fab Journey: one lot through the fab

A real wafer lot travels with a *traveler*: a card, now a database record, that lists every operation in order and gets signed off as each one finishes. The [Fab Journey](https://normansrule.github.io/transistor-foundry-expedition/fab.html) page is built around that idea, and from v1.12 the whole site borrows its look: a lot strip under the navigation, numbered steps, and a stamp that lands when you finish a section.

## The page

- **Seven scopes.** The hero lens dives from the planet (quartz sand, silicon dioxide) through the fab, the scanner, the wafer, one die and one gate-all-around transistor down to the silicon crystal. The wafer's die grid comes from the same `dieGrid` function as the Process Lab's yield model; the other scopes are schematic drawings.
- **Fab map.** Thirteen process areas from `data/fab_steps.json`, for three routes: a modern 2 nm-class gate-all-around flow, a teaching-cleanroom CMOS flow and the historical planar process. Opening an area shows what problem each step solves and how it works. Progress is kept in your browser only.
- **Route card.** The route's steps in an order that respects what each one requires (a topological sort of the `requires` lists).
- **Process theatres.** One per process, each with a computed cross-section. v1.12 shipped lithography; v1.13 adds thermal oxidation, ion implantation, plasma etch, deposition and chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP), v1.14 adds diffusion from a surface source (predeposition and drive-in, see [Chapter 25](25-real-cleanroom-run.md)), and v1.15 adds Czochralski crystal growth and the anneal, v1.17 adds damascene copper and wafer probe, v1.18 adds the nanosheet release and the historical planar process, and v1.19 adds wafer cleaning, whose chemistry stays conceptual.
- **Virtual Fab.** Write your own traveler. Each step is checked against the steps it requires and stamped OK or HOLD. Since v1.19 every step that passes runs the model behind its process theatre, and a two-column cross-section (the field under the mask and the window opened by lithography) is computed from the results; a yield estimate updates as you go. Since v1.20 it can use the settings you chose in the theatres instead of their defaults, so a hotter oxidation or a longer drive-in shows up in the cross-section. A dopant of the wafer's own type (boron into the p-type wafer) makes no junction, and a diffused junction spreads sideways under the oxide edge by about 0.8 x_j [@plummer2000]. Since v1.21 all three routes run through without a hold: lithography needs a clean wafer rather than an oxide, probing needs either metal step, and the 1959 route gains its own step, aluminium leads evaporated over the oxide and touching the junction through a contact window, as in Noyce's patent [@noyce_patent]. An implanted junction also spreads sideways, by the lateral straggle, taken equal to the vertical one [@plummer2000]: under the mask edge the dopant falls as ½N_peak erfc(y/√2σ), about 45 nm for the default phosphorus implant. Three buttons load the teaching, modern and 1959 routes. Since v1.22 a fourth button loads the real 2024 teaching-cleanroom run, a third setting runs it with that run’s own furnace recipes, and a table under the cross-section sets seven computed numbers beside what the run measured [@cleanroom_run_fab2024; @cleanroom_run_char2024]: Deal–Grove lands within 6 % of all three oxides, while the intrinsic-diffusivity model makes the source and drain ten times too resistive (four times since v1.23, when the run’s recipe lets the diffusivity rise with doping [@fair1981]), the vertical etch misses the wet etch’s undercut, and the aluminium film turns out about 216 nm thick from its sheet resistance and the bulk resistivity [@gall2016]. Since v1.24 the diffusion theatre also takes the drive-in ambient. Oxide growing on the surface injects silicon interstitials [@hu1974], and the diffusivity scales as f_I·S + (1 − f_I)/S with S = C_I/C_I* [@plummer2000]. That speeds boron and phosphorus and slows antimony. The run’s recipe assumes S = 3 for its oxidising drive-in, which deepens the junction by about half but hardly changes the sheet resistance.
- **Manuals 12 and 13 (v1.26).** Every roadmap area now has a manual. Manual 12 builds an oxide thin-film transistor on glass, the family with no crystal and no furnace [@nomura2004]. Manual 13 rebuilds the 1959 planar transistor, and its junction depth and breakdown voltages are read from the same model as the planar theatre, so the manual and the theatre cannot disagree [@sze_gibbons1966; @sze_gibbons_curv1966].
- **Thermal budget (v1.25).** Every hot step after doping keeps moving the junctions already made. The Virtual Fab keeps a Gaussian equivalent of each junction, with the same dose, peak and depth. A later oxidation, diffusion or anneal adds 2Dt to its variance using the intrinsic diffusivity [@plummer2000], and oxidation also eats into the silicon above it. An hour of dry oxidation at 1000 °C after the phosphorus diffusion pushes the window junction about 120 nm deeper. The same hour after a 20 keV implant takes the junction from 90 to 540 nm, which is why modern flows do their hot steps before the shallow implants and anneal them with a spike. Each traveler row shows the push, and a readout totals it.
- **Other process families.** A table compares eight ways of making transistors: the 1959 planar process, the 2024 teaching NMOS run, FinFET and gate-all-around logic, FD-SOI, SiGe BiCMOS, silicon carbide power devices, GaN HEMTs and IGZO thin-film transistors. Each is described by what it starts from, its defining step, its hottest step, its masks and what it makes. Silicon carbide needs implant activation anneals near 1600–1800 °C because its dopants hardly diffuse [@kimoto2014]. IGZO channels are deposited at room temperature on plastic [@nomura2004], and the GaN channel needs no doping because polarisation makes the electron sheet [@ambacher1999]. Every cell carries its reference, and a dash marks values with no public source, such as the mask counts of commercial processes.

## The lithography theatre

The theatre runs one exposure through seven stages: coat, soft bake, expose, post-exposure bake, develop, etch, and strip and inspect. Each stage has text at three depths (Beginner, Engineer, Process engineer). The cross-section is computed, not drawn:

1. **Aerial image.** The Physics Lab's Fourier-optics model ([Chapter 19](19-physics-lab.md)) gives the light intensity across three periods of a line/space mask for the chosen source (i-line, KrF, ArF immersion, EUV or High-NA EUV), illumination, pitch and defocus [@hopkins1953; @mack2007].
2. **Resist.** A positive resist clears wherever dose × intensity passes a threshold. The threshold, 9 mJ/cm², is a teaching value, not a real resist.
3. **Photon shot noise.** Light arrives as photons. A dose *D* of photons of wavelength λ delivers

   $$N = \frac{D\,\lambda}{hc}\,A$$

   photons to an area *A*, and Poisson counting makes the dose in that area fluctuate by 1/√N [@debisschop2017]. With the exact SI values of *h*, *c* and *e* [@codata2018], an EUV photon carries 91.8 eV and an ArF photon 6.4 eV, so at 30 mJ/cm² EUV delivers 20.4 photons per nm² against 291 for ArF. The theatre counts photons in 2 nm × 2 nm cells (about 82 for EUV at 30 mJ/cm², a fluctuation of ±11 %) and uses a fixed random field, so moving a slider never re-rolls the dice.
4. **Grid.** Forty rows of this noisy exposure make a small patch of wafer. The 2D section shows the middle row; the 3D isometric view shows all of them, so the edge roughness in 3D is the same randomness that moves the edges in 2D.
5. **Inspection.** For each row the theatre counts the printed spaces. Rows with a missing or extra space are bridges or breaks. The readouts give the mean printed critical dimension (CD) and its 3σ spread, and the theatre stamps PASS only when every row prints and the image contrast is above 0.15.

The two Rayleigh scaling laws are shown next to the simulation [@levinson2019; @mack2007]:

$$HP = k_1\,\frac{\lambda}{NA}, \qquad DOF = k_2\,\frac{\lambda}{NA^2}$$

The single-exposure limit is λ/2NA (k₁ = 0.25). Depth of focus uses k₂ = 1, so it compares sources rather than predicting a usable focus window. Raising NA from 0.33 to 0.55 shrinks it by (0.55/0.33)² ≈ 2.8.

Four failure modes load straight into the simulator: defocus, under-dose, stochastic edges at low EUV dose, and a pitch below the λ/2NA limit. Dipole illumination at its design pitch is a two-beam image and is very tolerant of focus in this scalar model, so the defocus demo switches to conventional light.

The light sources use the same λ and NA as the Physics Lab presets, and the evolution timeline follows [Chapter 7](07-lithography.md): mercury g-line and i-line, KrF and ArF excimer lasers, water immersion, self-aligned quadruple patterning at Intel 10 nm [@auth2017], EUV in production from 2019 [@bakshi2018; @asml_euv] and High-NA EUV in part of Intel's Panther Lake production [@intel_8k_2026].

**Safety.** Lithography is safety tier 1. The developer used in industry, tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH), is toxic; the page names it only conceptually and gives no recipe. Bake temperatures and times are left to the resist maker's data sheet.

**Photos.** The theatre shows the result, a CC0 die photograph of AMD's Zen 2 (TSMC N7) already logged in `data/images.json`. Tool photos stay hidden until `scripts/check_photo_licenses.py` confirms their licences.

## The process theatres

v1.13 adds five theatres that work like the lithography one: stages on a scrubber with text at three depths, controls that feed a model, a cross-section drawn from the model's output, live readouts, an equation panel, failure modes that load straight into the controls, tools, a cited timeline, metrology and what the model leaves out. Every number is cited or marked as an estimate. Hazardous process conditions (temperatures, concentrations, recipes) are not given; the controls are dimensionless or teaching values.

- **Crystal growth.** A Czochralski ingot pulled from a melt doped for a chosen seed-end resistivity. The crystal takes in only a fraction k of the dopant at the interface (boron 0.8, phosphorus 0.35, arsenic 0.3, antimony 0.023 [@trumbore1960]), so with a fraction f of the melt frozen the solid holds C_s = kC₀(1 − f)^(k−1) (the Scheil equation [@scheil1942; @plummer2000]). Resistivity follows through Caughey–Thomas mobility [@caughey1967], and the usable length is the part that stays within the customer's tolerance: about 69 % of a boron ingot at ±20 %, about 30 % of a phosphorus one. The history runs from Czochralski's 1918 metal crystals [@czochralski1918] through Teal and Little's germanium [@teal1950] to Dash's dislocation-free neck [@dash1959].
- **Wafer cleaning.** The chemistry is institution-only (safety tier 3) and is not described; the theatre follows what the clean is for [@kern1970; @kern1990]. Each particle-clean pass removes a fraction η of the particles, so N₀(1 − η)ⁿ are left. Their sizes follow the 1/d³ distribution of yield modelling [@stapper1983], so the share above a killer size d_k is (d₀/d_k)² with an inspection size d₀ of 50 nm (est.). Killers spread over a 300 mm wafer add a defect density that the negative-binomial model [@stapper1973] turns into lost dies: 2000 particles and one pass at 90 % leave 200, of which 50 are killers at 100 nm, and a 1 cm² die keeps 93 % yield; with no particle clean it keeps 53 %. Iron in the bulk is a recombination centre, τ = 1/(σₙ v_th N_Fe) with σₙ = 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm² [@istratov1999] and v_th = 1.1 × 10⁷ cm/s [@macdonald2001]: 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ gives about 180 µs and a diffusion length of 0.7 mm, 10¹² gives 1.8 µs.
- **Thermal oxidation.** The Deal–Grove model [@dealgrove1965] already used by the Process Lab: x² + Ax = B(t + τ), linear at first and parabolic later. The section shows the oxide growing into the silicon (44 % of its thickness comes from consumed silicon) for dry or wet oxygen, with the regime (reaction- or diffusion-limited) and the growth rate as readouts.
- **Ion implantation.** The Lindhard–Scharff–Schiøtt (LSS) range statistics [@plummer2000]: a Gaussian with projected range Rp and straggle ΔRp for boron, phosphorus or arsenic, against the substrate background, with the junction depth where they cross. Dots in the section are drawn from that profile with the fixed random field.
- **Anneal.** A shallow implant heated from 500 °C to a peak and back at a set ramp rate, with an optional hold. Diffusion depends only on the thermal budget Dt = ∫D(T(t))dt [@plummer2000; @fair1993]; each ramp adds ∫D dT / R, so a slow furnace ramp can matter as much as the hold. The equivalent time at the peak, Dt / D(T_p), broadens the implant Gaussian. A 150 °C/s ramp with a one-second hold moves a 5 keV boron junction from about 58 nm to about 64 nm; half an hour in a furnace moves it to about 420 nm.
- **Plasma etch.** Three openings (20, 40 and 120 nm) under a hard mask, etched to the endpoint of the widest one plus an over-etch. Neutral etchant reaches the bottom by molecular flow, so the rate falls with aspect ratio AR as R₀ / (1 + 3sAR/4), with reaction probability s [@coburn1989]: aspect-ratio-dependent etching, or RIE lag. Anisotropy sets how far the sidewalls recede, the mask erodes by its selectivity, and the stop layer below slows the front once the film clears. The optical-emission trace drops at the endpoint.
- **Deposition.** A trench filled by sputtering (PVD, s = 1), chemical vapour deposition (CVD, s = 0.05) or atomic layer deposition (ALD). Inside the trench the precursor obeys a diffusion-reaction balance n″ = (3s / 2w²) n, solved by the Thomas algorithm and re-solved as the walls grow; for a straight trench it matches the cosh solution exactly (a test checks this). High s starves the bottom and the mouth pinches off over a void; ALD's self-limiting half-cycles [@george2010] coat evenly at 0.11 nm per cycle. A gap narrower than a tenth of the trench at closure is called a seam, not a void.
- **Damascene copper.** A trench etched into the dielectric, lined with a barrier, seeded, plated bottom-up and polished [@kaanta1991; @edelstein1997]. Only the core (w − 2t)(h − t) conducts, and its resistivity rises above bulk through surface and grain-boundary scattering [@sondheimer1952; @mayadas1970] with mean free paths from Gall [@gall2016], as in the Physics Lab's interconnect model. A 20 nm copper line with a 2 nm barrier is about 11 µΩ·cm over the whole trench, against 1.68 for bulk; at 10 nm it is about 34, and barrier-free ruthenium (about 16) wins. The line's RC delay is the distributed Elmore term 0.38 rcL² [@elmore1948].
- **Probe, dice and bond.** The Process Lab's Monte Carlo wafer map (negative-binomial yield [@stapper1973]) is probed die by die, sawn, picked and bonded. Cost per good die is the wafer cost over the good dies, using a reported leading-edge wafer price [@tsmc_n2_wafer]. Fault coverage T sets how many bad dies escape: the defect level is 1 − Y^(1−T) [@williams1981], about 1000 parts per million for a 91 % yield at 99 % coverage and ten times that at 90 %.
- **Nanosheet release.** Silicon-germanium layers between three silicon sheets are etched out sideways from both ends until the fronts meet in the middle [@loubet2017]. The etch is selective but not perfect: if SiGe etches S times faster than silicon, every nanometre of lateral etch thins the exposed silicon by 1/S nm on each face, and the sheet ends, exposed longest, thin most. With 30 nm sheets 5 nm thick, S = 100 and a 20 % over-etch, the edges finish at 4.64 nm and the centre at 4.94; the effective width 2(w + t) per sheet over three sheets is 207 nm of an ideal 210. At S = 20 the edges fall to 3.20 nm. Without enough over-etch a SiGe core is left and the sheets are not free; at 60 nm the fronts travel twice as far and the edges thin to 4.28 nm.
- **The planar process (1959).** Hoerni's process [@hoerni1962]: an oxide mask grown over the wafer [@frosch1957], a window opened, boron predeposited and driven in under the oxide, so the junction meets the surface beneath the protecting oxide [@riordan1997; @noyce_patent]. The drive-in uses the diffusion theatre's model [@plummer2000]. Breakdown of a flat one-sided abrupt junction in silicon follows Sze and Gibbons, about 60 (E_g/1.1)^1.5 (N_B/10¹⁶)^−3/4 V [@sze_gibbons1966; @sze2006], and the curved edge of the window, a cylinder of radius about x_j, breaks down first by the factor √((r/W)² + 2(r/W)^(6/7)) − r/W [@sze_gibbons_curv1966], valid for r up to about the depletion width W. On 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ n-type silicon a one-hour drive-in at 1100 °C gives x_j = 2.05 µm, 347 V for the flat junction and 150 V (43 %) at the edge; four hours at 1150 °C deepen it to 6.5 µm and raise the edge to 208 V (60 %).
- **CMP.** Preston's law, removal rate = Kₚ P V [@preston1927], with the pad pressing only on the raised oxide until the step falls below a contact height, then sharing the pressure with the low areas [@stine1998]. Three regions with pattern densities 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 polish together; the dense one finishes last, and the spread of final heights is why fabs add dummy fill.

## Gate to silicon

"This is a NAND, and this is what it looks like in silicon." The section shows one logic gate six ways at once, for nine gates: the inverter, NAND2, NOR2, NAND3, NOR3 and the compound gates AOI21, OAI21, AOI22 and XOR2 (exclusive OR, built as an AOI22 fed with the inputs and their complements from two input inverters):

1. **Logic symbol, truth table and Boolean expression.** Choosing a row of the truth table sets the inputs.
2. **Transistor schematic**, drawn recursively from the gate's series/parallel expression. A static CMOS gate is a pull-down network of n-channel MOSFETs (nMOS) and a pull-up network of p-channel MOSFETs (pMOS) that is its dual: series nMOS become parallel pMOS and the other way round. Which transistors conduct comes from the Circuit Lab's switch model (`logic.py` / `logic.js`), and the path that drives the output is animated.
3. **Mask layout.** Each input is one gate finger crossing both strips of diffusion. Both strips must use the same finger order, and neighbouring transistors should share a source/drain region, so the generator searches for an order that is an Euler path through both networks at once (nodes are nets, transistors are edges) [@uehara1981]. A series network is then contacted only at its two ends; a parallel network has a contact between every pair of fingers. When an internal node turns up twice along a strip (in AOI21, AOI22, OAI21 and XOR2), a via and a metal-2 strap join the two regions. Numbered arrows mark the key dimensions, each cited or marked as an estimate. The planar cell uses Mead–Conway λ rules at λ = 90 nm (a 180 nm-class process) [@mead1980], with widths from logical effort: a unit nMOS is 4λ wide, series transistors are widened by the number in series and pMOS by 2 for their lower hole mobility [@sutherland1999]. Each planar device is drawn at its own width, so strips step where wide and narrow devices meet. A NAND2 measures 3.33 × 4.68 µm.
4. **The cell in 3D.** The same layout shapes extruded into a layer stack (diffusion or fins, gates, contacts, metal 1, via, metal 2), with a slider that pulls the layers apart. Heights are schematic.
5. **Cross-section** along a dashed cut line through the nMOS or pMOS row. Planar cells show silicided polysilicon gates on thin oxide, spacers over lightly doped extensions, silicided source and drain, tungsten contacts and copper metal 1; FinFET cells a fin under a high-k metal gate; nanosheet cells three stacked sheets wrapped by the gate with inner spacers. A glowing strip marks the electron or hole channel when a gate is on. Colours are the cross-section palette used by every figure in the repository.
6. **Size.** The FinFET cell uses TSMC N5's 51 nm contacted gate pitch and 28 nm metal pitch [@yeap2019]; the nanosheet cell uses the estimated 2 nm-class values in `data/nodes.csv` (45 nm and 21 nm). The NAND2 shrinks from 15.6 µm² to about 0.045 µm² (FinFET) and 0.032 µm² (nanosheet). For comparison, IBM's first stacked-nanosheet transistors were shown at 44/48 nm gate pitch and 12 nm gate length [@loubet2017].

Two larger blocks follow the same six views.

- **Full adder.** The 28-transistor mirror adder [@weste2010]. Its carry stage computes K̄ = NOT majority(A, B, Cin). Majority is its own dual, so the pull-up network can have the same series/parallel shape as the pull-down, mirrored, which is where the name comes from. The sum stage reuses K̄: S̄ = A·B·C + K̄·(A + B + C). Two inverters restore S and Cout. Each of the four stages is one unbroken strip of shared diffusion found by the same Euler-path search. The stages are separated by diffusion breaks, and the inputs and K̄ travel along pink metal-2 tracks to their gate fingers. The planar adder is 17.3 × 8.0 µm; the FinFET one is 1.02 × 0.45 µm.
- **6T static random-access memory (SRAM) cell.** Two cross-coupled inverters hold Q and Q̄, and two access transistors connect them to the bit lines BL and B̄L̄ when the word line is high. The pull-down transistors are twice as wide as the access transistors (a cell ratio of 2), so a read cannot pull the stored 0 up far enough to flip the cell [@seevinck1987]. The nMOS strip runs BL, access, Q, pull-down, ground, pull-down, Q̄, access, B̄L̄; the pMOS strip runs Q, pull-up, VDD, pull-up, Q̄. The page's state table shows that a read discharges the bit line on the 0 side. The drawn FinFET cell is 0.064 µm². A real N5 high-density cell is 0.021 µm² [@yeap2019], because foundry bitcells use rules that logic cells are not allowed: shared contacts, one fin per device, and gates and contacts drawn at the limits of the process.

Both blocks then get a seventh view, **tiled into an array**. Four adder cells sit side by side as a 4-bit ripple-carry adder. Each carry-out runs on metal 3 to the next cell's carry-in, and the page adds two 4-bit numbers stage by stage through the transistor networks, lighting each carry that is passed on. The answer is not settled until the carry has crossed every cell, which is why wide adders use carry look-ahead instead [@weste2010]. Sixteen SRAM cells form a 4 × 4 array with every other row mirrored, so that neighbouring rows share a power rail. The word line runs along each row and the bit-line pair down each column. Writing a bit or reading a row shows which bit lines fall and what each column's sense amplifier reads. For tiling to work the cell has to be drawn for it: the planar word line runs edge to edge in polysilicon, and in the FinFET and nanosheet cells the bit lines own metal 1 from top to bottom, so the power rails move up to metal 2. The page gives the density of the bare cells and compares it with the 0.021 µm² N5 high-density cell (about 48 Mbit/mm²) [@yeap2019].

The layouts are generated in Python by `gatecells.py` and `gateblocks.py` into `data/gate_cells.json`, and the page only draws them, so there is one source. NAND2, NOR2, AOI21, XOR2, the full adder and the SRAM cell are also written as GDS files to `figures/layout/` for KLayout.

## What the models leave out

- **Optics:** the aerial image is one-dimensional, scalar and thin-mask. Real masks have thickness, light has polarisation, and EUV masks are reflective with shadowing.
- **Resist:** a simple dose threshold. There is no acid diffusion, no dissolution kinetics and no standing waves; the post-exposure-bake blur in the animation is drawn, not computed.
- **Stochastics:** only incident photons are counted. Only a fraction is absorbed, and secondary electrons and acid blur add their own randomness, so real stochastic failures are worse than counting alone suggests.
- **Focus:** depth of focus uses k₂ = 1; real usable focus depends on the feature, the illumination and the allowed CD error.
- **Gate cells:** teaching drawings, not a cell library. Real cells add well taps, fill, cut-poly and many more design rules; the planar cell keeps a λ grid but is not checked against spacing rules; the 3D heights are schematic; real FinFET pMOS use SiGe channels; and the 2 nm-class pitches are estimates. The cross-section is not to scale. The adder ignores carry-chain sizing and the SRAM cell has no shared contacts, write-assist or array periphery; its state table treats the read as a clean digital event rather than a slow bit-line discharge sensed by an amplifier. The arrays leave out the row decoder, precharge circuits, sense amplifiers and well taps, and the carry routes are drawn over the cells rather than checked against the metal-3 rules.
- **Process theatres:** each model is one dimension or one region at a time. Etch has no ion shadowing, charging, bowing or polymer passivation, and a single reaction probability; deposition has no surface diffusion, re-emission or overhang from sputter angles, and treats the trench as a slit; CMP has no pad bending across the die, no dishing of metal and no erosion of the stop layer. Crystal growth uses the equilibrium segregation coefficient and a perfectly mixed melt, with no evaporation, radial variation or oxygen uptake [@bps1953]. The anneal has no transient enhanced diffusion [@eaglesham1994] and no activation limit, and cools at the same rate it heats. Rates are teaching values.
- **Release and planar:** the release etch rate is constant, with no dependence on germanium content, strain or the sheet's crystal planes; the planar edge uses the abrupt-junction curvature formula although a diffused junction is graded, and ignores the corners of a square window, which are spherical and break down lower still.
- **Cleaning:** no chemistry is modelled. Removal per pass is the same for every particle size, the 50 nm inspection size is an estimate, and only interstitial iron is counted among the metals.
- **Oxidation-enhanced diffusion:** one interstitial excess S for the whole drive-in, constant in depth and time, with interstitials and vacancies in local equilibrium. In a real furnace S follows the oxidation rate, falls with depth and fades as the oxide thickens, and the run’s S = 3 is assumed, not measured.
- **Thermal budget:** the profile after any later heat is a Gaussian of the original dose, not the solved concentration-dependent profile. There is no oxidation-enhanced diffusion during a later oxidation, and no segregation of dopant into the growing oxide.
- **Process families:** a qualitative table. Mask counts and peak temperatures of commercial processes are mostly not public, so those cells stay empty rather than estimated.
- **Virtual Fab:** one dimension per column, at each theatre's default settings. Resist and metal thicknesses are estimates, implant masking treats oxide and resist as if they stopped ions like silicon, diffusion is masked by any oxide at all, CMP has no pattern density, lateral spread is a fixed 0.8 x_j for diffused junctions and the straggle of the peak for implanted ones (an implant through a thin mask keeps the unmasked spread), the two vertical scales are also used sideways for that spread, and the nanosheets are a drawing.
- **Not simulated:** overlay, mask making, optical proximity correction (OPC) and multiple patterning.
